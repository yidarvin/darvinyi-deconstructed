# Carrie Mae Weems: one room, changing terms

Carrie Mae Weems's *Kitchen Table Series* (1990) makes recurrence do narrative work. The lamp, square frame, table, door, and back wall return so consistently that a new body, prop, gesture, or empty interval reads as a change in social relation rather than a change of scene. The twelve study plates here do not stand for the whole series; they are a selected route through couple scenes, friendship, grooming, mothering, and solitude. The overlays name only the concrete structures that make those changes legible: lamp axes, table planes, mirrors, cages, cards, touch, and the occasional barrier. For holdings-specific print information and the fair-use basis for these display copies, see the [source notes](../../content/carrie-mae-weems/sources.md).

## a stage that will not move

<Plate slug="carrie-mae-weems" id="01-man-and-mirror" label="Untitled (Man and Mirror), 1990" caption="The lamp, foreground mirror, and angled table establish a centered stage in which the mirror interrupts a frontal figure." />

The first image makes the room's grammar explicit. The hanging lamp, mirror stand, and woman’s face nearly share an axis; the mirror then blocks the torso that a conventional portrait might reveal. The table edge tilts just enough to make a shallow plane instead of a neutral platform. Its analyzer record is deliberately spare: edge density 0.0224, with values from p05 0.090 to p95 0.725, so the bright lamp and pale tabletop carry more weight than detail. The overlay’s mirror screen is not a claim about a reflected second face: it marks the object that turns direct address into an obstruction.

<Plate slug="carrie-mae-weems" id="02-man-smoking" label="Untitled (Man Smoking), 1990" caption="Cards, bowl, and table edges hold two differently oriented figures in a shared but unequal exchange." />

Here the two profiles do not resolve into a clean exchange of looks. Cards bridge the middle, a bowl presses forward at the lower edge, and the man’s larger foreground body redirects the table’s perspective toward the right. The overlay follows the actual right table edge rather than inventing a landscape horizon; its convergence marker explains why bottle, bowl, cards, and hands seem to meet in a compact social field. The grayscale palette runs from near-black #1b1b1b to #aaaaaa, with a modest 0.0270 edge density, leaving objects and posture to do the work.

<Plate slug="carrie-mae-weems" id="03-eating-lobster" label="Untitled (Eating Lobster), 1990" caption="A cage, shared food, and converging hand gestures make the table a circuit rather than mere furniture." />

In *Eating Lobster*, the circular cage counterweights the two diners while hands and food collect in the center. The line between their gestures is intentionally not called a gaze: what is visible is a meal organized through hands, plates, and a table. The lamp rim makes a bright horizontal threshold above them; the visual density rises here to 0.0400, enough for cans, cage wires, food, and fabric to keep the room active without breaking its staged calm.

<Plate slug="carrie-mae-weems" id="04-man-reading-newspaper-1" label="Untitled (Man Reading Newspaper, Triptych 1), 1990" caption="An open newspaper makes a literal screen across the table while each figure turns inward." />

The newspaper is a physical partition, not a metaphor supplied afterward. Its open edge crosses the shallow plane between the seated figures, while a cigarette hand and a hand at the cheek keep both bodies self-contained. The table boundary and the lamp/newspaper axis make that division architectural. With p05 at 0.078 and p95 at 0.706, the print has enough tonal reach to separate the dark foreground figure, pale paper, table, and illuminated lamp without rendering the distance sentimental.

## relation, redistributed

<Plate slug="carrie-mae-weems" id="05-woman-and-phone" label="Untitled (Woman and Phone), 1990" caption="A foreground telephone and its cord turn a long table into a field of distance." />

The telephone is close enough to dominate the lower frame, yet no one is visibly using it. Its cord travels toward the lower left while the curled figure sits beyond it, under the lamp’s vertical run. This is a useful reversal of pictorial priority: the foreground object gets the largest scale, but the figure remains the emotional pressure point. The relatively low 0.0128 edge density and p05–p95 range of 0.149–0.729 make the wide tabletop and blank wall unusually available as distance.

<Plate slug="carrie-mae-weems" id="06-girlfriends-1" label="Untitled (Girlfriends Triptych 1), 1990" caption="Three bodies form a compact triangle, and visible contact makes the room newly collective." />

The fixed set now has three bodies, but it is not simply fuller. One hand lands at the central woman’s shoulder and another reaches her forearm, creating a real chain of touch that the overlay follows. The table edge and left-side perspective line provide the room’s old grammar; the figures alter it by making the central body a point of contact rather than isolation. This plate's p05–p95 span of 0.059–0.718 and 0.0297 edge density keep faces, hands, glassware, and patterned cloth distinct inside the lamp’s pool.

<Plate slug="carrie-mae-weems" id="07-girlfriends-2" label="Untitled (Girlfriends Triptych 2), 1990" caption="Three profiles and a low cluster of objects make a new rhythm without assuming a shared gaze." />

The companion plate shifts the arrangement without abandoning the room. A left profile, central face, and right profile form an arc; glasses, ashtray, and plate occupy the quieter interval below. The overlay names that arc as a distribution of faces, not a three-way eye line. The analyzer’s p05 0.071 and p95 0.702 give the monochrome print a broad enough value range for the dark wall hanging, pale tabletop, and separate figures to remain distinct.

<Plate slug="carrie-mae-weems" id="08-woman-brushing-hair" label="Untitled (Woman Brushing Hair), 1990" caption="The brush contact and stacked bodies make grooming a visible two-person action beneath the lamp." />

The standing figure’s brush and hand meet the seated figure’s head, producing a precise small action inside a large, stable architecture. Their bodies stack on the lamp’s axis while the table recedes toward a right-side convergence. The diagram follows the brush path because it is visible; it avoids turning the image into a generic diagonal or making a claim about private feeling. Its p05–p95 values of 0.129–0.706 and very low 0.0137 edge density let the few graphic transitions (lamp rim, arms, heads, chair, table) speak with unusual clarity.

<Plate slug="carrie-mae-weems" id="09-woman-and-daughter-with-makeup" label="Untitled (Woman and Daughter with Makeup), 1990" caption="Two mirrors and parallel makeup gestures make adult and child comparable without turning them into one image." />

The large central mirror and the smaller hand mirror give the plate two different scales of self-presentation. Mother and daughter each bring a hand toward the face, but they do not visibly look at one another; the composition makes a parallel available without pretending it is a shared gaze. The lamp and large mirror form a measured central column, while the right table edge opens room for the child and her small mirror. Grays cluster in a restrained #323232-to-#a8a8a9 palette; the 0.0235 edge density keeps the mirrors’ circles and face contours clear.

## the room after company

<Plate slug="carrie-mae-weems" id="10-woman-standing-alone" label="Untitled (Woman Standing Alone), 1990" caption="A frontal body, two planted hands, and the lamp’s vertical run make an emptied table field feel deliberate." />

Standing at the table, Weems’s protagonist gives the room a direct frontal counterweight. Both hands spread across the table edge, while the lamp crowns the body on a near-central axis. The overlay’s narrow body trace is not a substitute for the figure; it clarifies how a small shift from seated to standing makes the unchanged room feel newly accountable. This is the smoothest plate in the selection: edge density 0.0088, p05 0.110, p95 0.651. The blank wall and pale table are therefore active planes rather than background.

<Plate slug="carrie-mae-weems" id="11-woman-feeding-bird" label="Untitled (Woman Feeding Bird), 1990" caption="A raised arm reaches toward the cage while chair, lamp, and table retain the room’s measured distance." />

The cage at left is a circular counterform to the standing woman at right. Her lifted arm reaches toward it, but the overlay carefully calls that a reach rather than claiming a particular contact with the bird. The chair arc cuts the lower body and table from the wall-mounted cage, giving a commonplace act of attention a composed distance. The value span is compressed here, from p05 0.227 to p95 0.612, so the cage outline, lamp, silhouette, and chair back become the primary readable structures.

<Plate slug="carrie-mae-weems" id="12-woman-playing-solitaire" label="Untitled (Woman Playing Solitaire), 1990" caption="Raised hands bracket a solitary figure over an irregular field of cards, with cage and glass holding the sides in balance." />

In the final plate, cards spread across the table but do not become a tidy pattern. The raised arms bracket the face, and the lamp-face axis gives the solitary figure a visual center while the cage and glass keep the sides from collapsing inward. The overlay marks the card field as a broad, irregular zone rather than narrating a game state. Its 0.0317 edge density and p05–p95 range of 0.157–0.678 explain the crisp legibility of individual cards against the smooth broad table.

## Exercises

<ExerciseCard n="01" title="Keep the room; change the relation">
Make four photographs from one fixed camera position at a kitchen table. Keep a lamp, table edge, and one wall feature in every frame. Change only the number of people, one prop, or the direction of one body. Compare which change most alters the picture’s social pressure.
</ExerciseCard>

<ExerciseCard n="02" title="Use an object as a divider">
Place a mirror, newspaper, phone, or glass between two people. Do not treat it as decoration. Move it until it either blocks a body, redirects a line of sight, or gives the table a new scale, then make one frame where the object is indispensable.
</ExerciseCard>

<ExerciseCard n="03" title="Let the table make depth">
Work with one overhead light and a rectangular tabletop. Keep the front edge low, let its side edges recede, and arrange hands or small objects near the convergence area. Check whether the perspective joins the figures or makes distance between them more visible.
</ExerciseCard>

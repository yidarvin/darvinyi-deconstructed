# Lorna Simpson: pictures that withhold, language that presses

Lorna Simpson’s 1985–89 photo-text installations make the interval between a
photograph and its language physically legible. A back, a cropped torso, a
lettered oval, or a repeated gesture does not settle into a conventional
portrait; plaques, letters, and spacing reframe what can be inferred. The
twelve works here are a focused route through that method, not substitutes for
the installations. Their web-sized display renditions are used for criticism and
composition teaching under the documented fair-use rationale in the [source
notes](../../content/lorna-simpson/sources.md).

## one body, several accounts

<Plate slug="lorna-simpson" id="01-waterbearer" label="Waterbearer, 1986" caption="Two pouring streams extend a back-facing body across a dark photographic field; the separate text field is an equal part of the work’s structure." />

*Waterbearer* begins with a refusal of the frontal portrait. Two opposed pours
extend the turned body, while the separate text field below presses on the image
without explaining it away. The analyzer records p05 0.016, p95 0.933, and
0.0181 edge density: the white dress and water hold sharply against the black
field, then yield to language as a second physical register.

<Plate slug="lorna-simpson" id="02-stereo-styles" label="Stereo Styles, 1988" caption="Two rows of back-of-head portraits and the narrow caption strip make difference accumulate across a strict serial format." />

Ten back-of-head Polaroids in *Stereo Styles* are not evidence to classify; they
are a rhythm that defeats classification. The overlay marks the two portrait rows
and the caption strip, not a false bilateral symmetry. The hairstyles vary
precisely where the grid would promise sameness. Its p95 of 0.973 and 0.0208
edge density keep the white wall active around the dark hair masses.

<Plate slug="lorna-simpson" id="03-gestures-and-reenactments" label="Gestures and Reenactments, 1985" caption="Six framed body fragments and uneven text plaques create a cinematic cadence without a single continuous scene." />

The cropped arms in *Gestures and Reenactments* hand the eye from one black frame
to the next. The overlay’s arm path is a sequence, not a literal line in space:
the governing geometry is recurrence and the pause beneath each unit. With p05
0.035, p95 0.973, and a denser 0.0307 edge field, black borders and white shirts
make every gap count.

## seriality changes the terms

<Plate slug="lorna-simpson" id="04-five-day-forecast" label="Five Day Forecast, 1988" caption="Five matching torso panels form an upper strip, while a separate lower rhythm of plaques prevents repetition from becoming simple equivalence." />

*Five Day Forecast* places five cropped bodies under small day labels and above a
larger plaque run. The repeated crossed-arm pose is never a neutral typology:
the lower language multiplies possible readings. Its shallow 0.0095 edge density
and p05–p95 span of 0.380–0.937 leave enough white interval for the small dark
word units to carry disproportionate pressure.

<Plate slug="lorna-simpson" id="05-youre-fine" label="You're Fine, 1988" caption="A reclining body is segmented by a four-panel strip and bracketed by unequal diagnostic and positional plaque stacks." />

A long, nearly clinical body strip becomes a site of pressure in *You're Fine*.
Feet, torso, head, and hand move through four panels, but the unequal word stacks
at either side prevent a neutral medical reading. The analyzer’s very low 0.0057
edge density is useful evidence: most of the frame remains pale and quiet, so
body, plaques, and embossed words carry the work’s visual weight.

<Plate slug="lorna-simpson" id="06-guarded-conditions" label="Guarded Conditions, 1989" caption="A rear-view frieze repeats one guarded body while staggered plaques make the lower edge an unstable second register." />

Six nearly matching figures make *Guarded Conditions* seem systematic at first;
clasped hands and repeated frame bars make that system visibly restrictive. The
plaque field below refuses a single clean baseline. The overlay follows the rows
of figures and plaques, not the gallery floor. Its 0.0323 edge density is among
the strongest here because frames, letters, hands, and plaque edges remain active.

<Plate slug="lorna-simpson" id="07-kid-gloves" label="Kid Gloves, 1989" caption="Five cropped figures turn changing gloved hands into the only moving event across an otherwise regular row." />

In *Kid Gloves*, heads are withheld so completely that hands become the
variation. The overlay follows the changing glove positions and separates them
from the caption plaques; it does not invent a gaze or a face. The pale warm
palette and 0.0131 edge density make the white gloves and arm crossings clear.

## crops, circles, and dividing words

<Plate slug="lorna-simpson" id="08-necklines" label="Necklines, 1989" caption="Three tall cropped panels are answered by two compact word blocks; the central scalloped neckline turns crop into a repeating contour." />

The tall frames in *Necklines* never offer a whole face or body. Mouth,
collarbone, fabric edge, and dark field become separate propositions. A central
scalloped neckline supplies one visible contour, while the two plaque blocks
tighten language around it. The analyzer finds p05 0.043, p95 0.937, and 0.0191
edge density: a spare but high-contrast arrangement.

<Plate slug="lorna-simpson" id="09-three-seated-figures" label="Three Seated Figures, 1989" caption="Three repeated seated-body crops are paced by plaques above and to either side, making the cut at chin and knee an active constraint." />

The middle panel in *Three Seated Figures* is not a privileged portrait; it is a
unit in a three-part cadence. Plaques at the top and sides ensure that the
repeated crop never resolves into neutral typology. The display view is tonally
pale (p05 0.494, p95 0.949) with 0.0142 edge density, keeping flesh, white dress,
frame boundaries, and ochre plaques distinct without a deep black ground.

<Plate slug="lorna-simpson" id="10-untitled-two-necklines" label="Untitled (2 Necklines), 1989" caption="Two circular neckline crops bracket a severe stack of words, making an apparent symmetry visibly conditional." />

*Untitled (2 Necklines)* is the clearest case for an axis: two circular crops
bracket a stacked word column. The circles do not dissolve into a mirror image;
the language deliberately interrupts their balance. With p05 0.361, p95 0.961,
and 0.0099 edge density, dark circular fields and small plaques remain legible
against a large white interval.

<Plate slug="lorna-simpson" id="11-dividing-lines" label="Dividing Lines, 1989" caption="A physical central divider holds two unequal backs between an upper and lower verbal run." />

The title of *Dividing Lines* is materially visual: a vertical join splits two
differently marked backs, while plaques extend the phrase above and below. The
vertical overlay identifies the seam, not a claim that the panels are mirrors.
The p05 0.137 and p95 0.996 span is nearly complete; its modest 0.0155 edge
density keeps attention on bodies, boundary, and lettering.

<Plate slug="lorna-simpson" id="12-easy-for-who-to-say" label="Easy for Who To Say, 1989" caption="Five lettered ovals turn withheld faces into a serial sentence whose lower plaques revise every unit." />

The final work replaces faces with lettered white ovals. A line through five panel
centers reveals a measured sequence; one representative oval shows how a
mask-like letter field blocks portrait recognition. Its high 0.0532 edge density
fits a dense array of frames, ovals, hair, and plaques. It is not a horizon or a
filmstrip: the lower labels make the sequence continually reconsider itself.

## Exercises

<ExerciseCard n="01" title="Build an interval, not a caption">
Make one portrait and one short phrase on separate pieces of paper. Move the
phrase farther from the image in three steps without changing either item. Notice
when the distance makes the phrase descriptive, contradictory, or a second voice.
</ExerciseCard>

<ExerciseCard n="02" title="Withhold the face">
Photograph five variations of a person’s back, hands, collarbone, or clothing.
Keep the camera position and framing fixed. Make the changing gesture, not a
facial expression, the only reason to move across the sequence.
</ExerciseCard>

<ExerciseCard n="03" title="Use a repeated plaque field">
Print three or five images at the same size, then place words beneath them in a
second row. Do not make the words a one-to-one caption. Test whether changing
their order, spacing, or alignment changes the relation among the pictures.
</ExerciseCard>

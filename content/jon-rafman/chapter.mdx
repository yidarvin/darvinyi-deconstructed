Jon Rafman's *Nine Eyes of Google Street View* makes selection visible as a photographic act. These scenes were not made by a photographer standing at the curb: the project selects uncanny motifs from Google Street View's already automated, panoramic record. That distance does not make composition irrelevant. It changes its stakes. A road, a seam, a raised arm, or an animal can become a decisive event because the machine's indifferent sweep has placed it inside a complete, sometimes awkward frame. The overlays name only visible structures in the supplied whole-frame web renditions; titles, credits, access routes, and the image-specific fair-use rationale are preserved in the [source notes](../../content/jon-rafman/sources.md).

## delayed recognition is part of the frame

The red-shirted figure in *Nacozari de Garcia* is neither centered as a portrait nor isolated against a clean background. ## Exercises

<ExerciseCard n="01" title="Let an interruption redirect a route">
Photograph a road, corridor, rail, or path with a clear directional pull. Wait for one small element (a person, animal, sign, or reflection) to contradict that pull. Make sure the route is still legible enough for the interruption to change it.
</ExerciseCard>

<ExerciseCard n="02" title="Make a figure emerge late">
Work in a dense field of foliage, shelves, traffic, or repeating architecture. Place or wait for a figure that is visible but not immediately isolated. Review the image at a small size: the composition succeeds when discovery is delayed without becoming confusion.
</ExerciseCard>

<ExerciseCard n="03" title="Treat an error as a boundary">
Use a panoramic seam, reflection, compression artifact, screen edge, or abrupt color change already present in your image. Do not add an effect. Ask whether the discontinuity divides the frame, reroutes attention, or reveals the image's own making.
</ExerciseCard>

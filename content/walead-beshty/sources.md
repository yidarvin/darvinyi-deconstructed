# Walead Beshty — source shortlist

This set follows the two photographic systems most useful for reading Beshty’s practice: the *Travel Pictures* made in the abandoned Iraqi Diplomatic Mission in Berlin and altered by repeated airport X-ray screening, and rule-bound darkroom photograms in which the paper, folds, light, and processing become the image’s subject. Whitney’s collection records supply seven publicly visible, complete-frame *Travel Pictures*; Thomas Dane Gallery’s artist page supplies three public display renditions of the photograms. Every retrieved file opened successfully and is at least 640px on its long edge.

**Rights basis:** **fair-use** for every selection. These are copyrighted contemporary works by Walead Beshty; the Whitney records identify the artist and collection credit, and the Thomas Dane Gallery work records identify the artist, title, date, and medium. Each assessment below is limited and transformative: this nonprofit book uses a credited, web-sized complete-frame copy for criticism, scholarship, and composition teaching. The whole frame is necessary to discuss the work’s spatial and material construction; each copy links to its record and does not replace an original, museum record, gallery offering, or licensed reproduction.

## Research context

Whitney identifies the *Travel Pictures* as chromogenic prints from 2006–2008 and credits its examples as gifts of the artist and Wallspace, New York. The project combines the abandoned mission’s interior with the airport screening system that the negatives passed through: the same transit network that moves the work becomes visible in its color and exposure. MoMA’s 2009 discussion of Beshty’s photograms explains that the artist exposes rolled color photographic paper to additive cyan, magenta, and yellow light under predetermined rules: https://www.moma.org/explore/inside_out/2009/11/18/walead-beshty-in-new-photography-2009/. Together the selections make composition a record of systems—architectural, logistical, chemical, and procedural—rather than a transparent view onto a scene.

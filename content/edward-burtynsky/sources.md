# Edward Burtynsky — source shortlist

Burtynsky’s long-running examination of altered landscapes makes industrial process legible at the scale of a picture: excavations, heaps, machinery, urban rebuilding, and aerially seen water systems become fields of repetition, seam, route, and immense but measured human intervention. This set traces that development from the early quarry photographs through *Water* and *Anthropocene*. All twelve files were downloaded directly from the public display images on the artist’s official [Photographs Index](https://www.edwardburtynsky.com/photographs-index). The index supplies the title, place, and date alongside each complete web-sized frame. Each JPEG opened successfully; all have a long edge of at least 768px.

**Rights basis:** **fair-use** for every selection. The images are contemporary copyrighted photographs by Edward Burtynsky (credit: © Edward Burtynsky; the artist’s official site is the source and rightsholder-facing record), and no open licence is asserted. Each individual assessment below uses a public, record-linked web display copy for nonprofit criticism, scholarship, and transformative composition teaching. The whole published frame is needed to analyze the pictured system’s spatial organization; a crop would remove the relation being taught. These credited copies are web-sized and link back to the artist’s record, so they do not substitute for an original print, artist-authorized sale, or licensed reproduction.

## Research context

The artist’s [project index](https://www.edwardburtynsky.com/photographs-index) groups the images into bodies of work including *Quarries*, *Oil*, *China*, *Water*, *Anthropocene*, and *African Studies*. The [Shipbreaking project statement](https://www.edwardburtynsky.com/projects/photographs/shipbreaking) explains Burtynsky’s decision to follow decommissioned single-hulled vessels to the dismantling yards of India and Bangladesh. This shortlist uses complete frames across those projects because the book’s subject is precisely the tension between visual order and the material systems that produce it.
